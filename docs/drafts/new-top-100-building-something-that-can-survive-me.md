# The New Top 100: Building something that can survive me

*Draft — the first in a series about the changes happening across Top 100, and why we're making them.*

There was a bit of a disaster in the Youth Shield this weekend.

Nine matches weren't played. Managers hadn't sent friendly requests, hadn't chased opponents, hadn't checked what was happening or, in some cases, hadn't been able to find their fixtures easily enough.

The resulting discussion has been lively.

Some of the criticism has been fair. The new Tournament Centre wasn't as easy to navigate as it should have been. One of our long-standing rules had become outdated because Soccer Manager had changed the way friendly matches are arranged. Both have now been fixed.

Some of the criticism has been rather less convincing. If two experienced managers know they have a knockout match and neither makes any attempt to arrange it, I'm not persuaded that one should qualify because the other manager's failure to do nothing was somehow greater than their own.

But I'm actually quite glad people are arguing about it.

It means they care.

When so many games weren't played, my first reaction was rather different. It felt as though 17 managers simply didn't care whether they played in the competition or not. The subsequent debate has demonstrated fairly emphatically that they do.

And underneath all the chuntering, it has exposed something much more interesting.

It has shown exactly why Top 100 is changing.

## The problem isn't really the Youth Shield

For years, an enormous amount of Top 100 has depended on one person.

Me.

That's partly my own doing. I've been running this game world since 2015. When something needs organising, fixing, publishing, checking or resolving, I've generally just done it.

A tournament needs setting up? I'll do it.

Fixtures need publishing? I'll do it.

Somebody hasn't arranged a game? I'll chase it.

There's a disputed result? Send it to me.

We need a draw? I'll organise it.

Someone forfeits? I'll work out what happens next.

Manager Awards need votes collecting? I'll build a form.

The form doesn't prevent duplicate votes? I'll check them.

Something on the website breaks? I'll fix it.

Someone can't find something? Ask David.

That works, until it doesn't.

It is exhausting. More importantly, after a while it stops being fun.

Top 100 is supposed to be something I participate in because I enjoy it. I want to manage my team, write stupid newspaper headlines, run competitions, build interesting things and enjoy the community around them.

I don't particularly want to spend my Saturday night adjudicating whether two grown men made sufficient attempts to send each other a friendly request.

And I can't do it forever.

## So we're building a Top 100 that doesn't need me

That's the bigger idea behind all the new websites and apps that have started appearing.

They aren't being built because everybody desperately needed another login.

They're being built so that information, rules and responsibility no longer have to pass through me.

The new Tournament Centre is a good example.

Managers can see the schedule, groups, tables, knockout draws, rankings, fair-play records and results themselves.

**My Matches** goes a step further. Sign in and it shows only the matches relevant to you — including whether **YOU SEND** the friendly request or **THEY SEND** it.

Only 22 of the 64 Youth Cup entrants created manager accounts this season. That's fine.

Signing up wasn't compulsory. It was an optional tool I created to make participating easier. Managers who prefer not to have an account can still look at the public fixtures and play exactly as they always have.

But we're beginning to see what becomes possible when a manager *does* have an account.

## From rules to infrastructure

The Youth Shield mess provides a pretty good example.

At the moment the rules say that arranging a match is a shared responsibility. The home manager sends the friendly request; if it doesn't arrive, the away manager contacts them.

That has traditionally required human judgement when something goes wrong.

It doesn't have to.

We've already started encoding some of our more peculiar tournament rules into the Tournament Centre. It can deal with forfeits. It understands two-legged ties and away goals. It can even calculate our fictional extra time rules.

And it works.

The next step could be to do the same with arranging matches.

A home manager could record that the friendly request has been sent. An away manager who hasn't received it could record that they've contacted their opponent. If there's a dispute, either can upload a screenshot.

Now we're no longer reconstructing on Sunday what everybody claims happened during the week.

The system knows.

One manager made a reasonable effort and the other didn't? Apply the appropriate forfeit.

Neither did anything? Neither benefits from the other's failure.

Something genuinely went wrong? Flag it for an organiser.

The rules haven't become more complicated. Their application has become **clearer, more consistent and less dependent on one person's judgement**.

That's the direction we're heading.

## Sharing the work

There's another important experiment already happening.

Pane Trifunov is running the latest Top 100 Regen tournament.

Previously, giving somebody else the ability to run a competition could mean giving them access to things they didn't need, explaining an assortment of manual processes, or still having me sitting behind the scenes doing half of it.

Now Pane can be an organiser for **his tournament**.

He gets the tools he needs. He doesn't get control over competitions he isn't running. The system handles the structure around him.

That might sound like a small technical improvement.

It isn't.

It means we've started separating **Top 100** from **the person who happens to administer Top 100**.

Other people can take responsibility.

Tournament organisers can run tournaments. Managers can arrange and report their own matches. The software can apply routine rules. Only the genuinely unusual cases need somebody to intervene.

Eventually, somebody else could run the whole thing.

I'm not announcing my retirement. Sorry.

But I should be able to retire.

Or go on holiday. Or get ill. Or become ridiculously busy. Or simply decide after umpteen seasons that somebody else can have the keys for a while.

A community that's been running this long shouldn't disappear because one person stops pressing the buttons.

## One manager, one account

This also explains another part of the New Top 100: manager accounts.

Again, the intention isn't to put Top 100 behind a login screen. Most of it should remain open.

Accounts are useful where **identity actually matters**.

Your matches. Your results. Your tournament registrations. Your actions in arranging a fixture.

And voting.

Managers have asked before for Manager Awards voting to be genuinely **one manager, one vote**.

We've tried WordPress polls. We've tried polling services. We've tried Google Forms. They all solve bits of the problem, but none really knows what a *Top 100 manager* is.

Now we do.

One manager can have one account. That account can be connected to their club and competitions. And when voting opens, the system can know that the person casting that ballot is an eligible Top 100 manager and that they haven't already voted.

That's a much better foundation than another Google Form and me checking the responses afterwards.

There's more to say about manager accounts, so I'll come back to them in the next article in this series.

## There will be bumps

We're changing quite a lot at once.

The old WordPress site is being replaced. More than a decade of Top 100 history has been moved. We have a new Tournament Centre, manager tools, voting, awards and statistics systems. Regen is becoming part of the same family. More is coming.

Not everything will be perfect first time.

This weekend proved that.

Some things that seem obvious when I build them won't be obvious to somebody encountering them for the first time. There will be bugs. There will be confusing bits. Soccer Manager itself will change something and make a perfectly sensible rule written years ago suddenly nonsensical.

Tell me.

The response shouldn't be *that's how the system works, tough luck*. It should be to listen, work out whether there's a real problem, and improve it.

That's exactly what happened this weekend.

Managers said fixtures were too difficult to find. We've made them easier to find.

Managers pointed out that an old 24-hour rule no longer made sense after changes to Soccer Manager. We've corrected it.

Managers questioned how responsibility for an unplayed fixture should be determined. We can make that clearer too — and eventually let the system record the evidence and apply the rule consistently.

That's how this should work.

## Less administration. More football.

Success isn't getting all 100 managers to create an account.

It isn't building the cleverest tournament website.

And it definitely isn't producing more rules.

A much better measure would be this:

**Nobody misses a match because they didn't know who they were playing, when they were playing, or what they were supposed to do.**

Managers spend less time trying to find things.

Organisers spend less time chasing people.

Decisions are fairer and more consistent.

More people can help run things.

And I don't have to be involved in every tiny transaction that keeps Top 100 moving.

If we get that right, the technology should gradually become the least interesting part of it.

We'll just have a game world that's easier to join, easier to understand, easier to organise and — hopefully — more fun to play in.

And, after more than a decade, one capable of carrying on without me.

That is the New Top 100.
